🎯 Module 06 · Feature Selection

Correlation Analysis

Measure the strength and direction of linear relationships between features and the target — a cornerstone of filter-based feature selection.

The Concept

How Correlation Works

The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) measures how linearly related two variables are. It ranges from -1 (perfect negative) through 0 (no relationship) to +1 (perfect positive).

In feature selection, we typically: (1) remove features with low correlation to the target, and (2) remove one of two features that are highly correlated with each other (redundancy).

r = Σ[(xᵢ - x̄)(yᵢ - ȳ)] / √[Σ(xᵢ-x̄)² · Σ(yᵢ-ȳ)²]
Real-World Examples

Correlation in Action

🏠 Real Estate
House Price Prediction
Area (sqft) has r≈0.85 with price — strong positive correlation, keep it. Number of bathrooms may correlate with number of bedrooms (r≈0.9) — one of them is redundant, drop it.
🏥 Medical
Disease Risk Factors
Blood pressure and heart rate may both correlate with cardiovascular disease. But if they also correlate with each other (r=0.8), including both adds noise and multicollinearity.

🔑 Key Terms
Pearson r
Pearson Correlation
Measures linear relationship strength. Range: [-1, 1]. Only for continuous variables.
Spearman
Spearman Rank
Correlation based on ranks, not values. Works for non-linear monotonic relationships.
Multicollinearity
Multicollinearity
When two features are highly correlated with each other — one becomes redundant.
VIF
Variance Inflation Factor
Detects multicollinearity. VIF > 10 means the feature is highly redundant.
